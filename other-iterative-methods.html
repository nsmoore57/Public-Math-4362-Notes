    Section 6.5 Other Iterative Methods for Linear Systems

    

    Subsection Weighted Jacobi

    Jacobi iteration can be slow. A common modification is weighted Jacobi, which averages the new Jacobi guess with the previous iterate.
    🔗
    For the usual splitting of a linear system into diagonal and off-diagonal pieces, the Jacobi update has the form
    \begin{equation*} D^{-1}\bigl(b-(L+U)x^{(k)}\bigr)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Weighted Jacobi uses a weight \(\omega\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x^{(k+1)} = \omega D^{-1}\bigl(b-(L+U)x^{(k)}\bigr) + (1-\omega)x^{(k)}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    When \(\omega=1\text{,}\) the second term disappears and the method is ordinary Jacobi. For smaller \(\omega\text{,}\) the new guess is averaged with the previous guess. This can damp oscillatory or overly aggressive Jacobi steps and often improves convergence.
    🔗
    A common practical value is
    \begin{equation*} \omega \approx \frac{2}{3}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    There are formulas for an optimal \(\omega\text{,}\) but they depend on eigenvalue information, such as a maximum eigenvalue, which can be expensive to compute. One can approximate that information, for example with the power method, but \(\omega=2/3\) is often a reasonable default.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Successive over-relaxation (SOR)

    A similar idea can be applied to Gauss-Seidel. This gives SOR, short for successive over-relaxation. It is essentially a weighted Gauss-Seidel method:
    \begin{equation*} x^{(k+1)} = (1-\omega)x^{(k)} + \omega D^{-1}\bigl(b-Lx^{(k+1)}-Ux^{(k)}\bigr)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The expression inside the weighted term is the Gauss-Seidel-style update: it uses new information for the components already updated and old information for the remaining components.
    🔗
    SOR can use values of \(\omega\) larger than one, and those choices can be useful in special cases. However, SOR may require more computation than it is worth for many systems; Gauss-Seidel or weighted Jacobi can be more efficient in practice.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Relaxation and stationary methods

    Jacobi, Gauss-Seidel, weighted Jacobi, and SOR are examples of relaxation methods. They are also stationary methods and are built from a fixed-point iteration idea. Informally, the iteration is arranged to look like
    \begin{equation*} x^{(k+1)} = g(x^{(k)})\text{,} \end{equation*}
    or a closely related update. The method repeatedly applies the same iteration rule until the approximations converge.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Krylov subspace methods

    More powerful iterative methods include Krylov subspace methods. These are more advanced theoretically and are not developed in detail here.
    🔗
    Important examples include:
    • conjugate gradient (CG)
      🔗
      🔗
    • GMRES, which stands for generalized minimum residual
      🔗
      🔗
    These methods are different from relaxation methods. They often converge faster and can be much more powerful, but they are also sensitive to preconditioning.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Preconditioning

    Preconditioning changes the form of the matrix system so that the numerical method has better convergence properties. Krylov methods often need a good preconditioner to realize their expected speedup.
    🔗
    Sometimes preconditioners are built using simpler methods such as Jacobi or Gauss-Seidel-type iterations. The idea is to transform an ill-conditioned system into a better-conditioned one, after which the Krylov method can converge much faster.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Further reference

    For a deeper treatment of large sparse systems, a standard reference is Yousef Saad’s book Iterative Methods for Sparse Linear Systems. Sparse systems are very large linear systems whose matrices contain mostly zeros, and they require different numerical techniques than dense matrix systems.
