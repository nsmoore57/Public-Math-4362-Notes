    Section 6.3 Convergence of Iterative Methods

    
    The iterative methods do not always converge. Let’s consider the following linear system
    \begin{equation} \begin{cases} 4x_1 - 3x_2 &= -1 \\ 2x_1 + 5x_2 &= 19 \end{cases}\tag{6.3.1} \end{equation}
    and the Jacobi iterative formulas are
    \begin{align*} x_1^{(k+1)} &= \frac{-1+3x_2^{(k)}}{4}\\ x_2^{(k+1)} &= \frac{19-2x_1^{(k)}}{5} \end{align*}
    Starting from the initial guess \(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}=(0,0)\text{,}\) the first 10 iterates are
    \begin{align*} x_1^{(1)} &= -0.250000, \quad & x_2^{(1)} = 3.800000\\ x_1^{(2)} &= 2.600000, \quad & x_2^{(2)} = 3.900000\\ x_1^{(3)} &= 2.675000, \quad & x_2^{(3)} = 2.760000\\ x_1^{(4)} &= 1.820000, \quad & x_2^{(4)} = 2.730000\\ x_1^{(5)} &= 1.797500, \quad & x_2^{(5)} = 3.072000\\ x_1^{(6)} &= 2.054000, \quad & x_2^{(6)} = 3.081000\\ x_1^{(7)} &= 2.060750, \quad & x_2^{(7)} = 2.978400\\ x_1^{(8)} &= 1.983800, \quad & x_2^{(8)} = 2.975700\\ x_1^{(9)} &= 1.981775, \quad & x_2^{(9)} = 3.006480\\ x_1^{(10)} &= 2.004860, \quad & x_2^{(10)} = 3.007290 \end{align*}
    After 6 steps, the iterates get close to the exact solution \(x_1=2\) and \(x_2=3\text{,}\) and stays there, indicating the iteration converges.
    🔗
    Now let’s swap the equations in the system (6.3.1) and obtain an equivalent linear system,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{cases} 2x_1 + 5x_2 &= 19 \\ 4x_1 - 3x_2 &= -1 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    and the Jacobi iterative formulas are
    \begin{align*} x_1^{(k+1)} &= \frac{19-5x_2^{(k)}}{2}\\ x_2^{(k+1)} &= \frac{-1-4x_1^{(k)}}{-3} \end{align*}
    Starting from the initial guess \(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}=(0,0)\text{,}\) the first 10 iterates are
    \begin{align*} x_1^{(1)} & = 9.500000, \quad & x_2^{(1)} = 0.333333\\ x_1^{(2)} & = 8.666667, \quad & x_2^{(2)} = 13.000000\\ x_1^{(3)} & = -23.000000, \quad & x_2^{(3)} = 11.888889\\ x_1^{(4)} & = -20.222222, \quad & x_2^{(4)} = -30.333333\\ x_1^{(5)} & = 85.333333, \quad & x_2^{(5)} = -26.629630\\ x_1^{(6)} & = 76.074074, \quad & x_2^{(6)} = 114.111111\\ x_1^{(7)} & = -275.777778, \quad & x_2^{(7)} = 101.765432\\ x_1^{(8)} & = -244.913580, \quad & x_2^{(8)} = -367.370370\\ x_1^{(9)} & = 927.925926, \quad & x_2^{(9)} = -326.218107\\ x_1^{(10)} & = 825.045267, \quad & x_2^{(10)} = 1237.567901 \end{align*}
    It clearly shows that the iteration diverges and Jacobi method does not work for it.
    🔗

    Definition 6.3.1.

    A square matrix is said to be strictly diagonally dominant if for every row of the matrix, the magnitude of the diagonal entry in a row is larger than the sum of the magnitudes of all the other (non-diagonal) entries in that row. More precisely, an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) is said to be strictly diagonally dominant if, for each \(i\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} |a_{ii}|> \sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}| \end{equation*}
    where \(1 \le i \le n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 6.3.2.

    The Jacobi method and Gauss–Seidel method converge if the coefficient matrix of a linear system is strictly diagonally dominant.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.3.

    Do you expect the Jacobi iteration in Example 1 and Gauss-Seidel iteration in Example 2 to converge? Why?
    🔗
    Solution.
    
    The coefficient matrix of the linear system in Example 1 and 2 is
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 3 & 1 \\ -3 & 10 & 2 \\ 1 & 7 & -15 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The matrix \(A\) is strictly diagonally dominant since
    \begin{align*} |7| & > |3| + |1|\\ |10| & > |-3| +|2|\\ |-15| & > |1|+|7| \end{align*}
    Therefore, by the theorem, both Jacobi iteration and Gauss-Seidel iteration for this linear system converge.
