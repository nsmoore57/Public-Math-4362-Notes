    Section 5.3 LU Decomposition

    In this section, we demonstrate that the steps involved in Gaussian elimination not only solve a linear system but also reveal a deeper structure of the matrix \(A\text{.}\) Specifically, the elimination process can be viewed as decomposing \(A\) into the product of two matrices: a lower triangular matrix \(L\) and an upper triangular matrix \(U\text{,}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} A = LU \end{equation*}
    This factorization, known as LU decomposition, is a powerful tool in numerical linear algebra. It allows for efficient solutions of linear systems, especially when multiple right-hand sides are involved, and it forms the foundation for many more advanced matrix algorithms. In the LU decomposition, \(U\) captures the row operations used to eliminate entries below the pivots, while \(L\) records the multipliers used in those operations. Rigorously, given a square matrix \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\text{,}\) LU decomposition factors it as:
    \begin{equation*} A = LU, \end{equation*}
    where \(L\) is a lower triangular matrix in the form of
    \begin{equation*} L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \ell_{21} & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \ell_{31} & \ell_{32} & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \ell_{n1} & \ell_{n2} & \ell_{n3} & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and \(U\) is an upper triangular matrix in the form of
    \begin{equation*} U = \begin{bmatrix} u_{11} & u_{12} & u_{13} & \cdots & u_{1n} \\ 0 & u_{22} & u_{23} & \cdots & u_{2n} \\ 0 & 0 & u_{33} & \cdots & u_{3n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & u_{nn} \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    Note that all diagonal entries of \(L\) are 1 and entries below the diagonal ( \(\ell_{ij}\) for \(i > j\) ) are the multipliers used during Gaussian elimination, and the matrix \(U\) comes from the result of Gaussian elimination.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.1.

    Find LU decomposition of
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ -1 & -6 & -21 \\ 3 & 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    
    Apply Gaussian elimination to reduce the matrix \(A\) into upper triangular form,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ -1 & -6 & -21 \\ 3 & 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_2 - \boxed{\frac{-1}{2}}R_1 \\ R_3 - \boxed{\frac{3}{2}}R_1 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ 0 & -4 & -16 \\ 0 & -3 & -8 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_3 - \boxed{\frac{-3}{-4}}R_2 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ 0 & -4 & -16 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The boxed multipliers form the lower triangular part of \(L\text{,}\) while the resulting upper triangular matrix defines the matrix \(U\text{,}\) namely,
    \begin{equation*} L=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -\frac{1}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{3}{2} & \frac{3}{4} & 1 \end{bmatrix},\quad U=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ 0 & -4 & -16 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose \(A\) is the coefficient matrix of the linear system
    \begin{equation*} A \boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{b} \end{equation*}
    By factoring \(A = LU\text{,}\) the system becomes
    \begin{equation*} LU \boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{b} \end{equation*}
    This can be viewed as two successive systems:
    \begin{align*} L\boldsymbol{c} &= \boldsymbol{b}\\ U\boldsymbol{x} &= \boldsymbol{c} \end{align*}
    The first system is solved using forward substitution, since \(L\) is lower triangular. The second is solved using backward substitution, as \(U\) is upper triangular.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.2.

    Use LU decomposition to solve the linear system
    \begin{equation*} \begin{cases} 2x_1 + 4x_2 + 10x_3 = -22 \\ -x_1 - 6x_2 - 21x_3 = 59 \\ 3x_1 + 3x_2 + 7x_3 = -17 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    
    By last example, the coefficient matrix \(A\) can be decomposed into \(LU\) as follows,
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ -1 & -6 & -21 \\ 3 & 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -\frac{1}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{3}{2} & \frac{3}{4} & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ 0 & -4 & -16 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} \end{equation*}
    Then, \(L\boldsymbol{c} = \boldsymbol{b}\) becomes
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -\frac{1}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{3}{2} & \frac{3}{4} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} -22 \\ 59 \\ -17 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    Solving this system using forward substitution, we obtain \(\boldsymbol{c}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} -22 \\ 48 \\ -20 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and thus the system \(U\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{c}\) becomes
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ 0 & -4 & -16 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} -22 \\ 48 \\ -20 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    Then solving this system for \(\boldsymbol{x}\) using backward substitution, we have
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 8 \\ -5 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
