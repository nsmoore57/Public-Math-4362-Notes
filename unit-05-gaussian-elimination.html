    Section 5.2 Gaussian Elimination with Backward Substitution

    The fundamental direct method for solving systems of linear equations is Gaussian elimination. In this section, we will cover two key steps of the process:
    • First, how to solve a linear system when the coefficient matrix \(A\) is already in upper triangular form. This step is known as backward substitution.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Second, how to transform a general linear system into an upper triangular form so that backward substitution can be applied. This transformation process is known as Gaussian elimination.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Backward Substitution

    Sometimes, the matrix \(A\) has a special structure that simplifies the solution process. For example, if \(A\) is an upper triangular matrix, then solving the system becomes straightforward.
    🔗

    Example 5.2.1.

    Solve the linear system
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 \\ 0 & -4 & -16 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix} -22 \\ 48 \\ -20 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    
    Note that the coefficient matrix \(A\) is upper triangular, we can solve the system using backward substitution. Starting from the last equation,
    \begin{equation*} 4x_3=-20 \implies x_3=-5 \end{equation*}
    Then substituting the value of \(x_3\) in the second equation,
    \begin{equation*} -4x_2-16x_3=48 \implies -4x_2-16(-5)=48 \implies x_2=8 \end{equation*}
    Similarly, solving the first equation for \(x_1\) by substituting the values of \(x_3\) and \(x_2\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} 2x_1+4x_2+10x_3=-22 \implies 2x_1+4(8)+10(-5)=-22 \implies x_1=-2 \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the solution is \(\begin{cases} x_1 = -2\\x_2 = 8\\x_3 = -5 \end{cases}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Gaussian Elimination

    Now, suppose the matrix \(A\) does not have any special zero structure. Gaussian elimination is a general method that uses elementary row operations to transform the system into an equivalent upper triangular form. Once in this form, the system can be solved using backward substitution.
    🔗
    
    Recall there are three types of elementary row operations:
    • Exchange two rows.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Multiply a row by a nonzero constant.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Add a multiple of a row to another row.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Gaussian elimination are as follows:
    \begin{align*} \begin{bmatrix} a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&\cdots&a_{1n}&b_1 \\ a_{21}&a_{22}&a_{23}&\cdots&a_{2n}&b_2 \\ a_{31}&a_{32}&a_{33}&\cdots&a_{3n}&b_3 \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots \\ a_{n1}&a_{n2}&a_{n3}&\cdots&a_{nn}&b_n \end{bmatrix} & \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_2 - \frac{a_{21}}{a_{11}}R_1 \\ R_3 - \frac{a_{31}}{a_{11}}R_1 \\ \vdots \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&\cdots&a_{1n}&b_1 \\ 0&a_{22}^{(1)}&a_{23}^{(1)}&\cdots&a_{2n}^{(1)}&b_2^{(1)} \\ 0&a_{32}^{(1)}&a_{33}^{(1)}&\cdots&a_{3n}^{(1)}&b_3^{(1)} \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots \\ 0&a_{n2}^{(1)}&a_{n3}^{(1)}&\cdots&a_{nn}^{(1)}&b_n^{(1)} \end{bmatrix}\\ & \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_3 - \frac{a_{32}^{(1)}}{a_{22}^{(1)}}R_2 \\ R_4 - \frac{a_{42}^{(1)}}{a_{22}^{(1)}}R_2 \\ \vdots \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&\cdots&a_{1n}&b_1 \\ 0&a_{22}^{(1)}&a_{23}^{(1)}&\cdots&a_{2n}^{(1)}&b_2^{(1)} \\ 0&0 &a_{33}^{(2)}&\cdots&a_{3n}^{(2)}&b_3^{(2)} \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots \\ 0&0 &a_{n3}^{(2)}&\cdots&a_{nn}^{(2)}&b_n^{(2)} \end{bmatrix}\\ & \qquad \vdots\\ & \xrightarrow{ \qquad \qquad } \begin{bmatrix} a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&\cdots&a_{1n}&b_1 \\ 0&a_{22}^{(1)}&a_{23}^{(1)}&\cdots&a_{2n}^{(1)}&b_2^{(1)} \\ 0&0 &a_{33}^{(2)}&\cdots&a_{3n}^{(2)}&b_3^{(2)} \\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots \\ 0&0 &\cdots&0&a_{nn}^{(n-1)}&b_n^{(n-1)} \end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
    The diagonal elements \(a_{11}\text{,}\) \(a_{22}^{(1)}\text{,}\) \(a_{33}^{(2)}\text{,}\) \(\ldots\text{,}\) \(a_{nn}^{(n-1)}\) are called pivot elements.
    🔗

    Example 5.2.2.

    Solve the linear system by Gaussian elimination and backward substitution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{cases} 2x_1 + 4x_2 + 10x_3 = -22 \\ -x_1 - 6x_2 - 21x_3 = 59 \\ 3x_1 + 3x_2 + 7x_3 = -17 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    
    Apply Gaussian elimination to reduce the augmented matrix of the system into an equivalent upper triangular form,
    \begin{align*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 & -22 \\ -1 & -6 & -21 & 59 \\ 3 & 3 & 7 & -17 \end{bmatrix} & \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_2 - \frac{-1}{2}R_1 \\ R_3 - \frac{3}{2}R_1 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 & -22 \\ 0 & -4 & -16 & 48 \\ 0 & -3 & -8 & 16 \end{bmatrix}\\ & \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_3 - \frac{-3}{-4}R_2 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 10 & -22 \\ 0 & -4 & -16 & 48 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 & -20 \end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
    After we get the equivalent upper triangular form, we can use backward substitution (see last example) to find the solution is \(x_1 = -2\text{,}\) \(x_2 = 8\text{,}\) and \(x_3 = -5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Pivoting Strategies

    To enhance the stability of Gaussian elimination, it’s important to choose pivot elements that are as large as possible. This minimizes the size of the multipliers, reducing the risk of significant round-off errors caused by finite precision. When a small pivot is multiplied by a large number, it can distort other rows and degrade accuracy. A common strategy is to compare the absolute values of the pivot and the entries below it, and then swap the current row with the one containing the largest value in magnitude, if different. This approach is known as partial pivoting. It works as follows: as the elimination proceeds for \(k = 1,\ldots,n −1\text{,}\) at each stage \(k\text{,}\) choose \(q = q(k)\) as the smallest integer for which
    \begin{equation*} \left|a_{qk}^{(k-1)}\right|=\max_{k\le i\le n} \left|a_{ik}^{(k-1)}\right| \end{equation*}
    and interchange rows \(k\) and \(q\text{.}\) Then proceed with the elimination process.
    🔗

    Example 5.2.3.

    Apply Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting to solve the following linear system.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{cases} x_1 - 2.5x_2 + x_3 +2.5x_4 = 9.5 \\ -x_1 + x_2 - 0.5x_3 + 8x_4 = 8.5 \\ -x_1 + 2.5x_2 + 3.5x_4 = -2.5 \\ 2x_1+x_2+4x_3+3x_4 = 7 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    
    Apply Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting to reduce the augmented matrix of the system into an equivalent upper triangular form,
    \begin{align*} & \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2.5 & 1 & 2.5 & 9.5 \\ -1 & 1 & -0.5 & 8 & 8.5 \\ -1 & 2.5 & 0 & 3.5 & -2.5 \\ 2 & 1 & 4 & 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\\ &\xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_1 \leftrightarrow R_4 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 4 & 3 & 7 \\ -1 & 1 & -0.5 & 8 & 8.5 \\ -1 & 2.5 & 0 & 3.5 & -2.5 \\ 1 & -2.5 & 1 & 2.5 & 9.5 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_2 - \frac{-1}{2}R_1 \\ R_3 - \frac{-1}{2}R_1 \\ R_4 - \frac{1}{2}R_1 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 4 & 3 & 7 \\ 0 & 1.5 & 1.5 & 9.5 & 12 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 & -1 & 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\\ &\xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_2 \leftrightarrow R_3 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 4 & 3 & 7 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 1.5 & 1.5 & 9.5 & 12 \\ 0 & -3 & -1 & 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_3 - \frac{1.5}{3}R_2 \\ R_4 - \frac{-3}{3}R_2 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 4 & 3 & 7 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.5 & 7 & 11.5 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 6 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\\ &\xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_3 \leftrightarrow R_4 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 4 & 3 & 7 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 6 & 7 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.5 & 7 & 11.5 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{ \begin{gathered} R_4 - \frac{0.5}{1}R_3 \end{gathered} } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 4 & 3 & 7 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 6 & 7 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 4 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
    🔗
    After we get the equivalent upper triangular form, we can use backward substitution to find the solution. Starting from the last equation,
    \begin{equation*} 4x_4=8 \implies x_4=2 \end{equation*}
    Then substituting the value of \(x_4\) in the last second equation,
    \begin{equation*} x_3+6x_4=7 \implies x_3+6(2)=7 \implies x_3=-5 \end{equation*}
    Similarly, solve the second equation for \(x_2\) and the first equation for \(x_1\text{,}\)
    \begin{gather*} 3x_2+2x_3+5x_4=1 \implies 3x_2+2(-5)+5(2)=1 \implies x_2=\frac{1}{3}\\ 2x_1+x_2+4x_3+3x_4=7 \implies 2x_1+\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)+4(-5)+3(2)=7 \implies x_1=\frac{31}{3} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Therefore, the solution is \(x_1 = \frac{31}{3}\text{,}\) \(x_2 = \frac{1}{3}\text{,}\) \(x_3 = -5\text{,}\) and \(x_4=2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Algorithm and Coding

    Let’s implement Gaussian elimination with backward substitution without pivoting in Python to solve a system of linear equations step by step. You can add partial pivoting by yourself.
    🔗
    
    import numpy as np
def gaussian_elimination(A, b):
    """Performs Gaussian elimination without pivoting"""

    A = np.array(A, dtype=np.float64)
    b = np.array(b, dtype=np.float64)
    n = b.size

    for k in range(n-1):
        for i in range(k+1, n):
            m = A[i, k] / A[k, k]
            A[i, k:] = A[i, k:] - m * A[k, k:]
            b[i] = b[i] - m * b[k]

    return A, b

def backward_substitution(U, y):
    """Solves Ux = y for upper triangular matrix U"""

    U = np.array(U, dtype=np.float64)
    y = np.array(y, dtype=np.float64)
    n = y.size
    x = np.zeros_like(y)

    for i in range(n-1, -1, -1):
        x[i] = (y[i] - np.dot(U[i, i+1:], x[i+1:])) / U[i, i]

    return x
    Now, let’s use this implementation to solve the linear system given in Example 2: 
    A=[[2,4,10], [-1,-6, -21], [3,3,7]]
b=[-22, 59, -17]

U, y = gaussian_elimination(A, b)
x = backward_substitution(U, y)
print("Solution x =", x)
    Running this code will produce: 
    Solution x = [-2.  8. -5.]
    🔗
    This confirms our implementation works correctly. Alternatively, you can solve the same system more easily using NumPy’s built-in solver: 
    x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
print("Solution x =", x)
    This gives the same output: 
    Solution x = [-2.  8. -5.]
    This built-in method is optimized and should be preferred for general-purpose use unless you’re explicitly learning or implementing algorithms manually.
