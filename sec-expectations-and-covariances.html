    Section 6.3 Expectations and Covariances

    Subsection Expectation

    
    One of the most important operations involving probabilities is that of finding weighted averages of functions. The average value of some function \(f(x)\) under a probability distribution \(P(x)\) is called the expectation of \(f(x)\) and will be denoted by \(\mathbb{E}[f]\text{.}\) For a discrete distribution, it is given by
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[f]=\sum_x P(x) f(x)\tag{6.3.1} \end{equation}
    so that the average is weighted by the relative probabilities of the different values of \(x\text{.}\) In the case of continuous variables, expectations are expressed in terms of an integration with respect to the corresponding probability density
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[f]=\int p(x)f(x) \, \mathrm{d}x\tag{6.3.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Sometimes we will be considering expectations of functions of several variables, in which case we can use a subscript to indicate which variable is being averaged over, so that for instance
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_x[f(x,y)]\tag{6.3.3} \end{equation}
    denotes the average of the function \(f(x, y)\) with respect to the distribution of \(x\text{.}\) Note that \(\mathbb{E}_x[f(x,y)]\) will be a function of \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We can also consider a conditional expectation with respect to a conditional distribution, so that
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_x[f|y]=\sum_x P(x|y)f(x)\tag{6.3.4} \end{equation}
    with an analogous definition for continuous variables.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Variance

    
    The variance of \(f(x)\) is defined by
    \begin{equation} \mathrm{var}[f]=\mathbb{E}\left[(f(x)-\mathbb{E}[f(x)])^2\right]\tag{6.3.5} \end{equation}
    and provides a measure of how much variability there is in \(f(x)\) around its mean value \(\mathbb{E}[f(x)]\text{.}\) Expanding out the square, we see that the variance can also be written in terms of the expectations of \(f(x)\) and \(f(x)^2\)
    \begin{equation} \mathrm{var}[f]=\mathbb{E}[f(x)^2]-\mathbb{E}[f(x)]^2\tag{6.3.6} \end{equation}
    🔗
    In particular, we can consider the variance of the variable \(x\) itself, which is given by
    \begin{equation} \mathrm{var}[x]=\mathbb{E}[x^2]-\mathbb{E}[x]^2\tag{6.3.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Another common measure of variability is standard deviation, represented by \(\sigma\text{,}\) which is the square root of the variance, namely,
    \begin{equation} \sigma=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[x^2]-\mathbb{E}[x]^2}\tag{6.3.8} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Covariance

    For two random variables \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) the covariance is defined by
    \begin{equation} \begin{split} \mathrm{cov}[x,y]&=\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\{x-\mathbb{E}[x]\}\{y-\mathbb{E}[y]\}]\\ &=\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[xy]-\mathbb{E}[x]\mathbb{E}[y] \end{split}\tag{6.3.9} \end{equation}
    which expresses the extent to which \(x\) and \(y\) vary together. If \(x\) and \(y\) are independent, then their covariance vanishes.
    🔗
    In the case of two vectors of random variables \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) the covariance is a matrix
    \begin{equation} \begin{split} \mathrm{cov}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}[\{\mathbf{x}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]\}\{\mathbf{y}^T-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}^T]\}]\\ &=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^T]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}^T] \end{split}\tag{6.3.10} \end{equation}
    🔗
    If we consider the covariance of the components of a vector \(\mathbf{x}\) with each other, then we use a slightly simpler notation \(\mathrm{cov}[\mathbf{x}] = \mathrm{cov}[\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}]\text{.}\)
